Tefteli: Meatballs in Tomato Sauce

Confession: I did not cook these. Max did, alone, in a kitchen I was nowhere near, on a night so cold that nobody was going out for anything, least of all celery. So I cooked with him by text. “Boil 100 g of rice, just on the stove.” Ok. “Ten minutes, lots of water.” Ok. “Sieve, cold water.” Ok. “Onion in the little mixer.” Ok. Twelve instructions, twelve Oks, which is more Oks than I get in person in a month. Then, out of nowhere, an actual question: would celery root do instead of stalks? That is how I knew the pan was really on. Dinner happened without me and I am told it was very good, which is both lovely and slightly annoying. This blog exists because my recipes kept dying in group chats, so here is this one, dragged out of iMessage and nailed to the wall.

Tefteli (тефтели) are the meatballs of my childhood, and of every childhood across the former Soviet Union, from kindergarten lunch to the works canteen. They are not really Russian, or not only: the name comes from the Turkic köfte family, the same root as Turkish köfte, Greek keftedes and Bulgarian kyufte, and the dish is made exactly this way in Ukraine, Belarus, Moldova and Kazakhstan. What sets them apart from their cousins, the kotlety, is the rice folded straight into the mince, which is why this version is sometimes called yozhiki, hedgehogs, after the grains that poke out as they cook. The rice makes them soft and a little plump, and it stretches a single pack of meat into a proper pot of dinner. I brown them first, then finish them in a tomato sauce with carrot and celery until everything is tender and the sauce has thickened around them.

Two things I insist on. I roll the meatballs in chickpea flour instead of wheat flour, which gives them a thin, nutty crust that holds up in the sauce. And I work a little water into the mixture, bit by bit, until I get the feeling it’s enough. That is what keeps them tender rather than dense. I portion them with an ice-cream scoop (the one with the blue handle) so they all cook at the same pace.

The Recipe

Serves: 4  •  Time: 1 hour 15 minutes

Meatballs

  • 500 g minced meat (beef, or mixed beef and pork)
  • 100 g round-grain rice
  • 1 onion
  • 1 egg
  • Salt and black pepper
  • 3–4 tbsp cold water
  • About 50 g chickpea flour, for coating
  • Neutral oil, for frying

Sauce

  • 2 carrots, diced
  • 2 stalks celery, diced (or a chunk of celery root)
  • 1 large can (800 g) whole peeled tomatoes
  • 250–300 ml vegetable broth
  • Salt and black pepper

Instructions

  1. Par-cook the rice: Boil the rice in plenty of salted water for about 10 minutes, until it is half-cooked but still firm. Tip it into a sieve and rinse under cold water.
  2. Chop the onion: Pulse the onion in a small food processor until very finely chopped, almost a paste.
  3. Make the mixture: Combine the mince, rice, onion, and egg in a large bowl and season generously with salt and pepper. Add cold water a spoonful at a time, kneading between additions, until the mixture is soft and holds together. Stop when it feels right.
  4. Shape: Portion the mixture with an ice-cream scoop and roll each portion into a ball.
  5. Coat: Sift the chickpea flour into a metal bowl and roll each meatball in it until lightly dusted all over.
  6. Brown: Heat a thin layer of oil in a large pan and fry the meatballs in batches until browned on both sides. They do not need to cook through. Transfer them to a wide pot.
  7. Fry the vegetables: In the same pan, fry the diced carrot and celery for a few minutes until they take on some colour.
  8. Make the sauce: Purée the tomatoes and stir in the broth and the fried vegetables. Taste and season with salt and pepper.
  9. Simmer: Pour the sauce over the meatballs, bring to a boil, then lower the heat and simmer gently for about 30 minutes until the rice is tender and the sauce has thickened.

Notes

  • For a crowd, double everything: two packs of mince, 200 g rice, two eggs.
  • No celery stalks? A piece of celery root, diced small, does the same job.
  • Chickpea flour keeps the dish gluten-free and gives a nicer crust, but plain flour works too.
  • Serve with mashed potatoes or buckwheat, and a spoonful of sour cream on top.
  • Like most stews, this is even better the next day.
